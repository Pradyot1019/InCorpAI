# Forming a Delaware C-Corporation

## Why Delaware {phase="Pre-Incorporation" clause="DGCL overview"}

Most venture-backed US startups incorporate as Delaware C-Corporations because the Delaware General Corporation Law (DGCL) is well developed, the Court of Chancery specialises in corporate disputes, and institutional investors expect the structure. A Delaware corporation can operate anywhere; it registers as a foreign corporation in the states where it actually does business.

## Step 1 — Registered agent {phase="Pre-Incorporation" clause="DGCL §131-132"}

Every Delaware corporation must maintain a registered office and registered agent in Delaware (DGCL §131). The agent receives service of process and official state mail. Commercial registered agents provide this for an annual fee.

## Step 2 — Certificate of Incorporation {clause="DGCL §102"}

The Certificate of Incorporation is filed with the Delaware Division of Corporations and signed by the incorporator. Under DGCL §102 it must state:

- The corporation's name, which must contain a word such as "Corporation", "Company", "Incorporated" or an abbreviation.
- The registered office address and registered agent.
- The nature of the business (a general "any lawful act or activity" purpose is permitted).
- The total number of authorised shares, their classes and par value.
- The name and mailing address of the incorporator.

Startups commonly authorise a large number of shares (for example 10,000,000) with a very low par value (for example $0.00001). The choice affects the annual franchise tax calculation, so see the annual-compliance document. Filing fees are set by the Division; check its current fee schedule.

## Step 3 — Organisational actions {phase="Post-Incorporation" clause="Initial board consent"}

1. The incorporator adopts an action appointing the initial board of directors (unless directors are named in the Certificate).
2. The board adopts bylaws, appoints officers (CEO, Secretary, Treasurer), sets the fiscal year, and authorises opening a bank account.
3. The board approves the issuance of founder stock under Restricted Stock Purchase Agreements, typically with a 4-year vesting schedule and 1-year cliff.
4. Each founder signs a Confidential Information and Invention Assignment Agreement (CIIAA) assigning pre-existing and future IP to the company.
5. The board adopts an equity incentive plan when ready to grant options.

## Step 4 — Section 83(b) election {phase="Post-Incorporation" clause="IRC §83(b)"}

Founders receiving restricted (unvested) stock should file an 83(b) election with the IRS **within 30 days of the stock grant**. The deadline cannot be extended. Filing the election means the founder recognises income at grant (usually near zero for founder stock) instead of as shares vest, when the value may be much higher. Keep proof of filing and give a copy to the company.

## Step 5 — EIN and federal registrations {phase="Post-Incorporation" clause="IRS Form SS-4"}

The corporation obtains an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS using Form SS-4. Applicants with an SSN or ITIN can apply online. Founders without an SSN or ITIN, including most non-US founders, apply by fax or mail.

## Step 6 — Foreign qualification and BOI {phase="Post-Incorporation" clause="Foreign qualification; FinCEN BOI"}

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Foreign qualification | Register as a foreign corporation in each state where the company has employees, offices or significant operations (for example California or New York) |
| Beneficial ownership (BOI) | Under FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule, companies formed in the United States are exempt from BOI reporting under the Corporate Transparency Act; only entities formed under foreign law and registered to do business in a US state must report |
| Fundraising | Companies selling securities under Regulation D Rule 506(b) or 506(c) file Form D with the SEC within 15 days after the first sale |
